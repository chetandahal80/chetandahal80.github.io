About

I work at the intersection of control theory and applied robotics: the kind of projects where a formal guarantee from a proof has to survive contact with real sensor noise, real hardware vibration and real people moving unpredictably nearby. At UNLV I'm building a multi-modal sensing pipeline of hyperspectral imagery and LiDAR point clouds, flown on both custom-built and commercial UAVs, to estimate wildfire fuel structure and moisture over wildfire-prone terrain, in collaboration with the Desert Research Institute.

I also work on independent projects like deriving a safety constraint from first principles for a mobile robot, assembling and tuning the quadrotor it flies on and running the tests that check whether the safety guarantee actually holds before calling it done. Before the MS, I spent a year and a half running factory operations for a 15-person production team in Nepal after finishing a Mechanical Engineering degree at Tribhuvan University. That's part of why I care as much about whether a system actually ships as I do about whether the math is right.

01Planning & ControlActiveJun 2026 – Present
Human-Aware Trajectory Planning with Formally Safe MPC
Replacing heuristic safety constraints in an MPC motion planner with Control Barrier Functions to give a wheeled mobile robot formally certified, collision-free navigation around moving humans.
ROS 2MPCCBFMotion PlanningHuman-Robot InteractionPython / C++
+
The challenge

Most MPC planners enforce safety around moving humans through heuristic, soft-constraint penalties. These give no formal guarantee that the robot will actually avoid collisions; they only discourage close passes through the cost function.

The goal: replace those heuristics with Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) that provide mathematically certified collision-avoidance guarantees, a provably stronger alternative, while keeping the planner real-time and smooth enough for shared human-robot environments.

My approach

Developing an MPC-based motion planning pipeline for a wheeled mobile robot, with real-time human state estimation via YOLOv8 detection and Kalman filter tracking feeding directly into the planner's safety layer.

Integrating discrete-time Control Barrier Function (DCBF) constraints into the MPC receding horizon formulation, extending formal safety guarantees to dynamic human obstacles using Kalman-predicted trajectories. Implemented and validated in ROS 2 simulation. A DPP-reformulated QP solver now runs the full pipeline at 17× real time and a 30-seed statistical sweep plus a deliberately non-straight-line human motion stress test have been used to actively look for and quantify where the safety guarantee is weakest.

02Planning & ControlMay 2026
CR3BP Spacecraft Control at Earth-Moon L2
Stabilising a spacecraft at an unstable Lagrange point under persistent disturbances, comparing pole placement, LQR and NSGA-II multi-objective optimisation to show that how you tune Q/R matters as much as which controller you pick.
MATLABLQRPole PlacementNSGA-IIPareto FrontServo-CompensatorCR3BP
+
The challenge

The Earth-Moon L2 Lagrange point is an unstable equilibrium: a spacecraft placed there will drift away without active control. The open-loop dynamics have an unstable eigenvalue at +2.1587, so the controller must first stabilise that instability.

On top of that, persistent sinusoidal disturbances act on the system at 1 rad/s. The controller must simultaneously stabilise the unstable dynamics and reject these disturbances asymptotically. A servo-compensator was augmented to the plant to enforce rejection via the Internal Model Principle, bringing the full system to 10th order. The deeper question this project asks: is Bryson's Method actually a good way to tune an LQR controller for mission-relevant performance? The answer, demonstrated systematically, is no.

My approach

Implemented four control architectures in order of increasing sophistication. Pole placement gives two isolated operating points but no understanding of the surrounding design space. LQR with Bryson's Method gives one physically motivated point but no way to know whether it's a good choice relative to other LQR designs.

An LQR Pareto front (alpha-sweep) reveals the complete tradeoff curve: Bryson's point sits at the high-effort end, while the knee point at α=0.0077 achieves comparable settling with 73% less effort. Finally, NSGA-II searches all 12 Q/R diagonal entries independently, finding controllers that alpha-scaling can never reach. A Luenberger observer handles state estimation throughout.

03UAV / AerialAug 2025
Autonomous UAV for Radiation Mapping in GPS-Denied Environments
End-to-end autonomy pipeline for mapping radiation in indoor, GPS-denied spaces using SLAM and ROS.
ROSPX4 SITLGazeboHector SLAMA* / DWA PlanningRVizGrid Maps
+
The challenge

In nuclear facilities or post-disaster environments, humans cannot safely enter radiation-contaminated spaces. A UAV could map these environments but GPS is unavailable indoors and radiation sensors must be fused with spatial data to produce a meaningful map, not just point readings.

The hard parts: making the UAV localize itself without GPS, avoiding obstacles in cluttered indoor spaces and overlaying sensor data on a 2D grid map in real time.

My approach

Built the autonomy stack in ROS, integrating PX4 SITL with Gazebo for realistic simulation of flight control, sensors and autonomy logic. Hector SLAM provides GPS-free localisation and maintains the 2D occupancy grid used for both mapping and obstacle detection.

On top of that grid, an A* planner handles global path planning toward the current goal, while a Dynamic Window Approach (DWA) local planner replans in real time around obstacles the global plan didn't anticipate. Radiation readings are georeferenced against the SLAM map and rendered as a colour-coded 2D grid map in RViz, updated live as the UAV flies. Addressed sim-to-real challenges including TF frame consistency, sensor noise and timestamp alignment across the ROS graph.

04UAV / AerialResearchApr 2026
LiDAR-Inertial SLAM on Custom Quadrotor (FAST-LIO)
Real-time onboard 3D mapping and state estimation deployed on a self-built quadrotor using an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano.
FAST-LIOLiDAR-Inertial SLAMJetson Orin NanoQuadrotor3D MappingROS 2
+
The challenge

Running 3D LiDAR-inertial SLAM fully onboard a UAV, without offloading to a ground station, is computationally demanding. The challenge was integrating a tightly-coupled SLAM algorithm (FAST-LIO) with limited onboard compute, on a custom-built quadrotor with real vibration and sensor noise.

Additionally, the UAV itself had to be assembled and tuned to fly stably enough that SLAM could produce clean maps, since hardware and software are tightly coupled here.

My approach

Assembled and tuned the custom quadrotor frame, ESCs and flight controller from scratch, iterating until the platform flew stably enough for SLAM. This hardware-software co-tuning is rarely discussed but is critical, as vibration and wobble directly corrupt LiDAR point clouds.

FAST-LIO, a tightly-coupled LiDAR-inertial odometry algorithm, was then integrated and configured for real-time operation on the Jetson Orin Nano. It fuses LiDAR point clouds with IMU data at high frequency to estimate pose and build a dense 3D map entirely onboard, with no ground station involvement.

05Deep LearningDec 2025
Deep Learning Pipeline for Hyperspectral NDVI Prediction
A Hybrid 3D-2D U-Net with Transformer encoders that maps high-dimensional hyperspectral imagery to vegetation health indices with R² of 0.9692.
PyTorchU-NetTransformersHyperspectral ImagingRegressionDeep Learning
+
The challenge

Hyperspectral cameras capture hundreds of spectral bands per pixel, creating 3D input tensors that are far richer and far harder to process than standard RGB images. The task: predict NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), a 2D continuous map of vegetation health, from these high-dimensional inputs.

Standard 2D CNNs discard spectral structure. Standard 3D CNNs are too expensive. The model needed to capture both spectral depth and spatial context efficiently.

My approach

Designed a Hybrid 3D-2D U-Net: 3D convolutions in the encoder extract spectral-spatial features across all bands, then a 2D decoder maps these to a dense NDVI output, capturing spectral depth without the full cost of a 3D network throughout.

Transformer encoders were integrated into the CNN bottleneck to capture global spatial context alongside local feature extraction. The training loop includes data augmentation, global min-max normalisation and validation-based checkpointing to prevent overfitting on the limited hyperspectral dataset.

06Planning & ControlJul 2024
3D Human Tracking: YOLOv8 + Kalman Filter + RGB-D
Multi-target 3D human tracking for mobile robot safety, fusing depth camera detection with a Kalman filter and optimal data association.
ROS 2YOLOv8Kalman FilterRGB-D CameraData AssociationPython
+
The challenge

For a robot to safely navigate around people, it needs more than "there's a person at pixel (x, y)." It needs stable 3D position estimates across multiple people, accounting for sensor noise, occlusions and the fact that people come and go from the scene.

Naive detection-only approaches flicker and lose track identity, making them unreliable for safety-critical use. The challenge was building a tracker that stays stable across time, even under noisy depth readings and partial occlusions.

My approach

YOLOv8 detects people in the RGB image. For each detection, the corresponding depth value back-projects the 2D bounding-box centre into a 3D position in the robot frame. A multi-target Kalman filter maintains smoothed estimates of each person's 3D position and velocity over time.

The hardest part is data association: when there are multiple detections and tracks, which detection belongs to which track? The Jonker-Volgenant algorithm, an efficient optimal assignment solver, resolves this at each timestep, enabling stable track identities even in crowded scenes.

07Embedded / EdgeNov 2025
Embedded ML for Hand-Tool Ergonomics Monitoring
A TinyML classifier running on an STM32 microcontroller that detects unsafe wrist posture during hand-tool use in real time, with 94% accuracy on the MCU.
TinyMLSTM32MEMS IMUEdge AIClassificationMEMS Studio
+
The challenge

Repetitive awkward wrist postures during hand-tool use are a leading cause of musculoskeletal disorders in manufacturing workers. Existing solutions require expensive motion-capture setups or clinician observation and neither is scalable to a factory floor.

The goal: build a wearable, real-time ergonomic monitor that runs entirely on a cheap microcontroller, with no cloud, no latency and no battery-draining wireless streaming.

My approach

Built a wearable prototype using an STM32 microcontroller and a MEMS IMU, developed in MEMS Studio. The IMU captures wrist and forearm motion during tool use and the data was used to train a TinyML classifier to recognise operation type and posture.

The model was quantised and deployed directly onto the MCU, running inference in real time without any wireless streaming or cloud dependency. The classifier distinguishes between safe and unsafe ergonomic postures at the point of use.

10ControlJul 2022
Perimeter-Defense Game: One Attacker vs. One Defender
MATLAB simulation of an optimal pursuit-evasion game where an attacker tries to breach a circular perimeter and a defender tries to intercept it, using differential game theory to derive and visualise the winning regions.
MATLABDifferential Game TheoryPursuit-EvasionOptimal ControlGame Theory
+
The problem

A defender moves along the perimeter of a circular target region. An attacker starts outside and tries to reach the perimeter without being intercepted. The key parameter is the speed ratio v = vattacker / vdefender. When the attacker is slower, can it still find a guaranteed winning strategy?

This is a two-player zero-sum differential game: both players act optimally and in opposition. The challenge is deriving closed-form optimal strategies and the exact boundary that separates winning initial conditions from losing ones.

My approach

Based on Shishika & Kumar (CDC 2018), I simulated the two-player game from first principles in MATLAB. The defender's optimal strategy is to track the attacker's angular position at maximum speed. The attacker's optimal strategy is to head toward the tangent point on the perimeter that maximises its lead over the defender.

The winning-region boundary is an involute of the circle, derived from the game's value function V. I simulated state trajectories for multiple initial conditions to verify that the theory holds: the attacker wins if and only if it starts inside the involute region.
